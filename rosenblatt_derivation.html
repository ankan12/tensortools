Rosenblatt Derivation

Rosenblatt transformation for a normal distribution

This section derives the Rosenblatt transformation first for a simple multivariate normal random vector and then for a tensor-variate normal random variable. The tensor result is the same normal calculation applied using the Kronecker-structured tensor covariance.

Rosenblatt transformation

Let

\[ X=(X_1,\ldots,X_p)^\top \]

be a continuous random vector with joint distribution function \(F(x_1, \dots, x_k)\).

We define the Rosenblatt transformation as follows:

\[ \begin{aligned} U_1 &=F_{X_1}(X_1),\\ U_2 &=F_{X_2\mid X_1}(X_2\mid X_1),\\ U_3 &=F_{X_3\mid X_1,X_2}(X_3\mid X_1,X_2),\\ &\ \vdots\\ U_p &=F_{X_p\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{p-1}} (X_p\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{p-1}). \end{aligned} \]

If the assumed joint distribution is correct and its parameters are known, then

\[ U_1,\ldots,U_p \stackrel{\mathrm{ind}}{\sim} \operatorname{Uniform}(0,1). \]

The important point is that each random variable is transformed using its distribution conditional on all the random variables that came before it.

Two-dimensional normal example

Suppose

\[ \begin{pmatrix}X_1\\X_2\end{pmatrix} \sim N_2\left[\begin{pmatrix}\mu_1\\\mu_2\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2\\ \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 & \sigma_2^2 \end{pmatrix} \right]. \]

Step 2.1: Transform the first coordinate

The marginal distribution of the first coordinate is

\[ X_1\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2). \]

Therefore,

\[ Z_1=\frac{X_1-\mu_1}{\sigma_1} \]

is standard normal, and the first Rosenblatt coordinate is

\[ U_1 =F_{X_1}(X_1) =\Phi\left(\frac{X_1-\mu_1}{\sigma_1}\right) =\Phi(Z_1). \]

Step 2.2: Find the conditional distribution of the second coordinate

For a bivariate normal distribution,

\[ X_2\mid X_1=x_1 \sim N\left( \mu_2+\rho\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}(x_1-\mu_1), \ \sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2) \right). \]

After subtracting the conditional mean and dividing by the conditional standard deviation, we obtain

\[ Z_2 = \frac{ X_2-\mu_2-\rho(\sigma_2/\sigma_1)(X_1-\mu_1) }{ \sigma_2\sqrt{1-\rho^2} }. \]

Conditional on \(X_1\), this quantity is standard normal. Thus the second Rosenblatt coordinate is

\[ \begin{aligned} U_2 &=F_{X_2\mid X_1}(X_2\mid X_1)\\ &=\Phi\left[ \frac{ X_2-\mu_2-\rho(\sigma_2/\sigma_1)(X_1-\mu_1) }{ \sigma_2\sqrt{1-\rho^2} } \right]\\ &=\Phi(Z_2). \end{aligned} \]

Under the assumed bivariate normal model, \(Z_1\) and \(Z_2\) are independent standard normal variables. Consequently, \(U_1\) and \(U_2\) are independent uniform variables.

3. Write the same calculation using a Cholesky factor

Now let

\[ X\sim N_p(\mu,\Sigma), \]

where \(\Sigma\) is positive definite. Let

\[ \Sigma=LL^\top \]

be its lower-triangular Cholesky decomposition. The lower-triangular form is important because it preserves the sequential conditioning used by the Rosenblatt transformation.

Define

\[ Z=L^{-1}(X-\mu). \]

Equivalently,

\[ X=\mu+LZ, \qquad Z\sim N_p(0,I_p). \]

Because \(L\) is lower triangular, the first few coordinates of \(X\) satisfy

\[ \begin{aligned} X_1 &=\mu_1+L_{11}Z_1,\\ X_2 &=\mu_2+L_{21}Z_1+L_{22}Z_2,\\ X_3 &=\mu_3+L_{31}Z_1+L_{32}Z_2+L_{33}Z_3. \end{aligned} \]

More generally,

\[ X_j = \mu_j+ \sum_{k=1}^{j-1}L_{jk}Z_k+ L_{jj}Z_j. \]

Step 3.1: Condition on the preceding coordinates

Once \(X_1,\ldots,X_{j-1}\) have been observed, the corresponding values \(Z_1,\ldots,Z_{j-1}\) are known from forward substitution. Therefore,

\[ X_j\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{j-1} \sim N\left( \mu_j+\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}L_{jk}Z_k, L_{jj}^2 \right). \]

Step 3.2: Standardize using that conditional distribution

The conditional standardized value is

\[ \frac{ X_j-\mu_j-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}L_{jk}Z_k }{L_{jj}} =Z_j. \]

It follows that the \(j\)th Rosenblatt coordinate is simply

\[ \boxed{U_j=\Phi(Z_j)}, \qquad Z=L^{-1}(X-\mu). \]

Thus the complete normal Rosenblatt transformation can be summarized as

\[ \boxed{ U = \Phi\left{L^{-1}(X-\mu)\right}, } \]

where \(\Phi\) is applied separately to every coordinate.

This is not just arbitrary whitening. Using the lower-triangular Cholesky factor makes each \(Z_j\) exactly the standardized residual from the conditional distribution of \(X_j\) given the preceding coordinates.

Rosenblatt transformation for a tensor-variate normal distribution

4. Specify the tensor-normal model

Let \(\mathcal X\) be a \(D\)-way tensor with dimensions \(p_1\times\cdots\times p_D\). Suppose

\[ \mathcal X \sim \operatorname{TVN} (\mathcal M,\Sigma_1,\ldots,\Sigma_D), \]

where \(\mathcal M\) is the mean tensor and \(\Sigma_d\) describes covariance along tensor mode \(d\).

Let

\[ x=\operatorname{vec}(\mathcal X), \qquad \mu=\operatorname{vec}(\mathcal M). \]

Using the usual column-major vectorization convention,

\[ x \sim N_p(\mu,\Sigma), \qquad p=\prod_{d=1}^D p_d, \]

with

\[ \Sigma = \Sigma_D\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma_1. \]

This equation is where the complete tensor covariance structure enters the Rosenblatt transformation.

5. Obtain the tensor-normal Cholesky factor

For each mode, take the lower-triangular Cholesky decomposition

\[ \Sigma_d=L_dL_d^\top. \]

Define

\[ L=L_D\otimes\cdots\otimes L_1. \]

Using the Kronecker-product identity

\[ (A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD), \]

we have

\[ \begin{aligned} LL^\top &=(L_D\otimes\cdots\otimes L_1) (L_D^\top\otimes\cdots\otimes L_1^\top)\\ &=(L_DL_D^\top)\otimes\cdots\otimes(L_1L_1^\top)\\ &=\Sigma_D\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma_1\\ &=\Sigma. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, \(L\) is the lower-triangular Cholesky factor of the full vectorized tensor covariance.

6. Standardize the tensor observation

Define

\[ z = L^{-1}(x-\mu). \]

Under the tensor-normal model,

\[ z\sim N_p(0,I_p), \]

so its coordinates are independent standard normal variables. The tensor-normal Rosenblatt values are therefore

\[ \boxed{ u_j=\Phi(z_j), \qquad j=1,\ldots,p. } \]

Equivalently, all steps can be written together as

\[ \boxed{ u = \Phi\left[ (L_D\otimes\cdots\otimes L_1)^{-1} \left{ \operatorname{vec}(\mathcal X)- \operatorname{vec}(\mathcal M) \right} \right]. } \]

7. Equivalent mode-by-mode tensor calculation

The same standardization can be performed without explicitly constructing the large Kronecker product. Let

\[ \mathcal E=\mathcal X-\mathcal M. \]

Then whiten the residual tensor along every mode:

\[ \mathcal Z = \mathcal E \times_1 L_1^{-1} \times_2 L_2^{-1} \cdots \times_D L_D^{-1}, \]

where \(\times_d\) denotes multiplication along mode \(d\). With compatible vectorization and mode-product conventions,

\[ \operatorname{vec}(\mathcal Z) = (L_D\otimes\cdots\otimes L_1)^{-1} \operatorname{vec}(\mathcal E) =z. \]

Finally, apply \(\Phi\) element by element:

\[ \mathcal U=\Phi(\mathcal Z). \]

This mode-by-mode form uses all of the tensor covariance matrices while avoiding construction of a potentially very large full covariance matrix.

8. What should be observed under a correct model?

For each tensor observation, the derivation produces

\[ u_1,\ldots,u_p. \]

If the tensor-normal model and its parameters are correct, then these values should be

\[ u_1,\ldots,u_p \stackrel{\mathrm{ind}}{\sim} \operatorname{Uniform}(0,1). \]

A diagnostic can therefore examine:

  1. Uniformity: histograms, uniform Q–Q plots, or a uniformity statistic;
  2. Independence within a tensor: correlations or other dependence measures among the transformed coordinates; and
  3. Independence across observations: dependence among transformed values from different independently sampled tensors.

When \(\mathcal M\) and the covariance matrices are replaced by estimates from the same data, the transformed values are not exactly independent uniforms in finite samples. For a formal goodness-of-fit test, the null distribution of the diagnostic statistic should therefore be obtained using a parametric bootstrap that repeats both model fitting and transformation.

9. Summary of the calculation

For one tensor observation:

  1. Subtract the fitted or known mean tensor: \(\mathcal E=\mathcal X-\mathcal M\).
  2. Compute a lower Cholesky factor \(L_d\) for every mode covariance \(\Sigma_d\).
  3. Whiten \(\mathcal E\) along all modes to obtain \(\mathcal Z\).
  4. Vectorize \(\mathcal Z\) in the same order used by the model.
  5. Apply the univariate standard-normal CDF to every entry: \(u_j=\Phi(z_j)\).
  6. Under the correct model with known parameters, the resulting values are independent \(\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)\) variables.
